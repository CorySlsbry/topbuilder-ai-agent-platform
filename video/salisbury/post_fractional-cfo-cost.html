What does a fractional CFO cost for a construction company?

A fractional CFO for a construction company costs $3,000 to $9,000 per month for an ongoing retainer. Diagnostic-only work runs $2,500 to $10,000 as a one-time fee, and a defined cleanup or build-out project runs $5,000 to $15,000. Hourly arrangements exist too, usually $150 to $450 per hour, but most contractors do better with a fixed monthly fee. Hourly billing punishes you for asking questions, which is the opposite of what a CFO relationship should reward.

Why is a fractional CFO so much cheaper than a full-time hire?

A fractional CFO is cheaper because you share one experienced person across several companies instead of paying one company's entire salary, bonus, benefits, and payroll tax. A full-time construction CFO costs $170,000 to $360,000 per year all-in, per CFMA (2025). A fractional retainer at $5,000 per month is $60,000 per year for the same caliber of judgment on the slice of time you actually need.

The CFMA numbers are worth sitting with. Smaller contractors pay a CFO an average of about $173,000 all-in, built from a base near $142,249 plus a bonus near $30,853 (CFMA, Construction Compensation Trends). Larger contractors push total compensation toward $357,000 (CFMA, Executive Compensation Survey). A full-time controller, a more junior role, still costs $130,000 to $200,000 per year all-in. None of that includes recruiting, the months a search takes, or the risk of a bad hire.

Most contractors at $1M to $25M do not have $250,000 of CFO-level work to do every year. They have a few high-stakes decisions, a recurring need for clean numbers, and a constant low-grade worry about cash. A fractional CFO is sized to exactly that.

What does a fractional CFO actually do for a contractor?

A construction fractional CFO turns your messy financial reality into decisions you can defend. The core work is job costing, work-in-progress reporting, a forward-looking cash forecast, and bank-ready financial statements. A bookkeeper records what already happened. A CFO tells you what it means and what to do next.

Construction makes this harder than it is in almost any other industry. Revenue arrives in draws and is held back by retainage. Costs land on jobs weeks before they are billed, or after. Profit on a project is recognized over months using percentage-of-completion, not when the check clears. This is exactly why the field has its own financial association: CFMA exists because contractor accounting does not look like normal accounting. A generalist CFO who has never managed WIP will miss the things that sink contractors.

The day-to-day deliverables usually include a job-cost report you can trust, a WIP schedule that shows over- and under-billings, a 13-week cash forecast, and a monthly close that produces statements a lender or a bonding company will accept. Done well, that package is what stands between a profitable-on-paper contractor and one who runs out of cash mid-project.

The work also includes the calls a contractor cannot delegate to software. Should you take the bigger job that ties up cash for four months, or two smaller ones that turn over faster? Is this the year to buy the equipment outright, finance it, or rent it? How much backlog can you safely carry before payroll outruns collections? A bookkeeper cannot answer those. A spreadsheet cannot either. Those are CFO judgments, made against clean numbers, and they are where the fee earns its keep. The reporting is the table stakes; the decisions are the product.

Is a fractional CFO worth it for a $1M to $25M contractor?

For most contractors in this range, yes, because the cost of not knowing your numbers is far larger than the fee. The average general contractor nets only 5% to 6% margin, while top performers pull 10% to 12% (industry benchmarks widely reported across construction-finance sources, 2026). The gap between average and best-in-class is usually not a revenue problem. It is a cost-recognition and cash-timing problem, which is precisely what a CFO fixes.

Material costs have made this more urgent. Construction input prices rose 9.6% year-over-year as of May 2026, per an Associated Builders and Contractors (2026) analysis of Bureau of Labor Statistics data, with a single-month jump of 2.6%, the fastest since the pandemic. When inputs move that fast, an estimate built on last quarter's prices quietly turns a profitable job into a losing one. A CFO who watches job costs in real time catches that before it compounds across a backlog.

Think of the fee as a percentage of the problem it solves. A $5,000-per-month retainer is $60,000 a year. On a $5M contractor, recovering one point of margin is $50,000. Tightening one slow draw cycle or catching one under-billed change-order package can clear the annual fee by itself. The question is not whether you can afford a CFO. It is whether you can afford to keep guessing.

There is a second, quieter return that never shows up on an invoice: the cost of cash you cannot see coming. Contractors fail more often from cash timing than from a lack of profit. Work is done and money is owed, but retainage is held, a draw is slow, and payroll comes every two weeks regardless. A 13-week cash forecast is cheap insurance against that exact failure. It turns "I think we are fine" into a dated schedule of inflows and outflows you can act on three months out. For a contractor carrying a backlog, knowing the week cash gets tight is worth more than almost any single tax move, because it is the difference between arranging a line of credit calmly in advance and scrambling for it in a crisis.

When should a contractor hire a fractional CFO?

The clearest trigger is when the size of your decisions outgrows the quality of your numbers. That usually happens somewhere between $1M and $5M in revenue, when payroll, material purchasing, and multiple concurrent jobs make gut-feel accounting dangerous. If you cannot answer "which of my jobs made money last quarter" within a day, you are already past the point where a CFO pays for itself.

Other common triggers: you are bidding bigger work and a bonding company wants clean, reviewed statements; you are planning to sell or take on a partner and need the books to survive due diligence; or you keep being profitable on paper while the bank account says otherwise. Each of these is a CFO problem, not a bookkeeping problem.

You do not need to wait until you can afford a full-time hire. That is the entire point of the fractional model. You can start with a diagnostic, see the leak list, and scale the relationship up only if the value is obvious.

What is the difference between a fractional CFO, a controller, and a bookkeeper?

They sit at three different altitudes. A bookkeeper records transactions. A controller makes sure those records are accurate and the monthly close is clean. A fractional CFO uses the clean records to look forward: cash strategy, job-level profitability, financing, and the calls that move the business. You need all three functions; the question is who does each and how much that costs.
