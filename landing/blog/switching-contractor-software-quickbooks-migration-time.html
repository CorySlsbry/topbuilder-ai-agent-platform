How Long Does Switching Off Your Current Field Software Actually Take?

Top Builder AI Published August 30, 2026 Updated August 30, 2026 ~12 min read Switching & Migration

Longer than the sales call said, usually, but not for the reason most owners assume. A small-business software switch runs about 4 to 6 weeks elapsed from kickoff to go-live in the real world, and a mid-market platform with more integrations and users runs 10 to 12 weeks plus another 2 to 4 weeks of stabilization, per Rework's SaaS buying framework guide. That gap between "sold in an hour" and "live in six weeks" is almost never the new software's fault: it's the data migration, cleaning up years of QuickBooks history, reconciling what a field platform tracked against what the books actually say, that eats the calendar. Below: the real industry timelines, what QuickBooks' own export tool can and can't do, whether an AI touching the ledger is something to actually worry about, and what a penny-verified migration looks like when it's built to be fast on purpose.

This is written for the owner who has already decided the current stack isn't working but is stuck on "I don't have a week to deal with switching." The math below is what actually determines the timeline, not the vendor who talks fastest on the demo call.

How long does switching contractor software actually take?

It depends almost entirely on how much data has to move, not on how complicated the new software looks. Per Rework's SaaS buying framework guide, a smaller tool typically lands in 4 to 6 weeks elapsed, while a mid-market platform with 50,000 to 500,000 records, several integrations, and 50 to 200 users runs 10 to 12 weeks to go-live plus another 2 to 4 weeks of stabilization after that. A finance/ERP-level switch runs longer still, 16 to 24 weeks in the same guide's estimates.

A one-or-two-truck shop switching field software sits closer to the small end of that range by record count alone, which is exactly why a multi-month migration quoted for that size of business is a red flag, not a sign of thoroughness.

Why do most software switches take longer than the sales call promised?

Because the sales call prices the software, not the migration. Per the same Rework guide, training costs are the single largest real switching cost, ahead of the software itself, followed by the cost of migrating and cleaning the actual data, and smaller organizations spend proportionally the most of anyone on that data-migration step. The guide also cites a sobering number: roughly 70% of SaaS migrations fail to deliver the expected savings on a three-year net-present-value basis, a gap that traces back to underestimating exactly that step at the sales stage.

That's the pattern behind almost every "I don't have time to switch" objection: it isn't fear of a new interface, it's a correct read that most vendors don't price or plan for what actually takes the time.

What actually happens to your QuickBooks history when you switch platforms?

It doesn't come along cleanly by default, which is the part most switching conversations skip. Per SaasAnt's QuickBooks Online export guide, QuickBooks Online's own built-in export tool has real limits: "some transactions and lists may not export fully," and exported "data may require additional cleanup before it's usable," with the built-in options lacking the filtering needed for a genuinely complete pull. That's Intuit's own native tool, not a competitor's complaint about it, and it's exactly why third-party migration tools and done-for-you services exist at all: a plain export-and-import rarely survives a real trial balance intact.

A contractor who has years of job history, customer records, and vendor bills sitting in QuickBooks should assume "export" means "a starting point that needs verification," not "a finished migration."

Can your CPA keep working the way they already do after you switch?

Yes, if the new platform is built for it rather than treating the accountant as an afterthought. Top Builder AI's own QuickBooks bridge module, shipped as a real feature rather than a roadmap promise, includes an Accountant Sync that pushes a one-way feed into the CPA's own existing QuickBooks account, so the accountant never has to log into a new system, learn a new chart of accounts, or change how they file. The module's own internal framing is blunt about the point: "your books leave with you, always."

That one design decision removes what is often the real veto power in a switching decision: a CPA who refuses to change tools can keep using the one they already know, on the exact same cadence, while the contractor runs the field and job-costing side somewhere better suited to it.

Is AI going to mess up the books if it's touching the ledger?

That fear is reasonable, and finance professionals share it broadly, which is exactly why the honest answer is "the AI should never be doing the math." Per insightsoftware's 2026 research on AI adoption in finance, 60% of finance professionals point to "stronger policies and guardrails, better training, and clearer governance frameworks" as what would actually increase their confidence in AI, and 75% say explainable AI, being able to see why a number came out the way it did, would increase confidence further. Separately, per Journal of Accountancy's coverage of Maximor's CFO survey, two-thirds of the 100 CFOs surveyed said human oversight of agentic AI in finance is extremely or very critical to ensuring accuracy.

The right architecture answers that directly rather than arguing with it: deterministic, tested code computes every number, and the AI's only job is narrating the result in plain business language, with a person approving every change before anything posts to the ledger. If a platform can't explain that split clearly, that's the actual thing to be worried about, not "AI" as a category.

What does migrating into Top Builder AI actually involve?

Two separate tracks, handled differently on purpose. The QuickBooks side is a shipped, deterministic migration: the incoming trial balance is checked per account against QuickBooks to the penny, any difference is itemized rather than absorbed into a rounding line, and the resulting ledger can be exported back out at any time as an IIF file or a QuickBooks journal CSV, the same export path whether a shop is arriving or, eventually, leaving.

Pulling job and cost history out of ServiceTitan, Jobber, or a similar field platform is handled as a done-for-you migration by a real person rather than an automated self-serve import button. That's a deliberate choice, not a missing feature: a fully automated field-data import risks folding years of job-level detail into a single lump-sum opening balance with no one, including the CPA, ever seeing the itemized version.
