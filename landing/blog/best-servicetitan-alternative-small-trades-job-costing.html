So why isn't ServiceTitan the right fit for a small crew?

ServiceTitan is a genuinely excellent platform, for the business it is built for, which is an established, multi-technician operation. It is aimed at larger shops, and its pricing reflects that: published 2026 per-technician analyses put it in the range of roughly $245 to $500 per technician per month, typically on an annual contract, and independent implementation guides commonly cite an onboarding fee somewhere between $5,000 and $50,000, with a configuration process that runs weeks to months before go-live. None of that is a scandal; it is what enterprise field-service software costs and takes. The mismatch is buying it as a two- or three-truck shop, paying enterprise rates for dispatch and inventory depth you will mostly never touch, and spending months onboarding a system sized for a business three times yours. Right tool, wrong size.

What do Jobber and Housecall Pro leave out?

The tools actually sized for a small shop are Jobber and Housecall Pro, and they are good. Per their own pricing pages, Jobber runs roughly $21 to $280 per month and Housecall Pro roughly $59 to $299 per month, both far more approachable than ServiceTitan, both strong at scheduling, dispatch, quoting, and invoicing. If all a shop needs is to book work and get paid, either is a reasonable pick, and this piece is not going to pretend otherwise.

The wall they hit is the same one, and it is the reason so many contractors still cannot answer a simple question at month-end. Both hand accounting off to QuickBooks. Neither produces real job costing on its own, so the moment an owner wants to know what a specific job made after true labor and material cost, the answer lives in a different system, or in a spreadsheet, or in a bookkeeper's head. The field tool knows what you invoiced. It does not know what the job cost you.

Can't I just use QuickBooks for job costing?

The instinct is "the field tool plus QuickBooks covers it." In practice it does not, because QuickBooks was built for general small-business accounting, not construction job costing. It has no native work-in-progress (WIP) schedule. It has no real cost-code structure for splitting a job cleanly into labor, materials, and subs. It does not track committed costs from open purchase orders, so a job can look profitable right up until the material bills land. Shops paper over all of this with spreadsheets and a bookkeeper reconciling after the fact, which is exactly how "what did this job make" turns into a question answered three weeks late, if at all. This is not a QuickBooks failing so much as a category mismatch: it is a great general ledger being asked to do a job-costing job it was never designed for.

What does "real job costing" actually mean?

Real job costing is simply this: every hour and every part lands on the specific job, at its true cost, so profit is knowable per job instead of only per month. The catch most software misses is that the true cost of labor is not the hourly wage. It is the burdened rate: wages plus payroll taxes, plus workers' comp by class code, plus the overtime split at 40 hours. A journeyman billed to a job at his base wage looks cheaper than he is; costed at his burdened rate, the job's real margin appears. Add materials posted to the job, committed costs pulled from open purchase orders before the bill even arrives, and actuals compared to the estimate, and the owner finally gets a straight answer to the only question that matters: what did this job make.
